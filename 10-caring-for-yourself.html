When a son, a daughter, a spouse, or a sibling goes into residential care, the rest of the family often runs on adrenaline. Someone has to manage the house, the shop, the children, the questions, and the fear of a phone call at night. That pace feels necessary. It is also how relatives become ill, short-tempered, or unable to be of any use when the person comes home. If someone you love is at a Nasha Mukti Kendra in Ranchi, looking after yourself is not a luxury. It is part of the same piece of work.

What exhaustion looks like in a family

You may be sleeping badly, skipping meals, or replaying old arguments at two in the morning. You may be irritable with children who have done nothing wrong, or unable to concentrate at work. Some relatives swing between hope and bitterness several times a day. Some begin to use their own "small" comforts more heavily: more tobacco, more alcohol, more late-night scrolling. If that is happening, name it to yourself honestly. You are not failing. You are carrying too much without a pause.

Children in the house need a grown-up who is still present, not only a grown-up who is managing a crisis. An hour of ordinary attention, a regular meal, or a walk to school can matter more than a perfect explanation of addiction.

Small, specific things that help

You do not need a new philosophy. You need a few repeatable habits. Eat at roughly the same times. Sleep in a bed, not in a chair by the phone. Ask one trusted person for a concrete task, such as collecting a child or sitting with a parent, rather than asking everyone for advice. Limit the family WhatsApp thread that reviews every rumour. Write down questions for the next call with the centre, instead of holding them all in your head.

If you are unsafe, or if violence has been part of the story, your safety comes first. Staying in a dangerous situation is not proof of loyalty. Speak to someone who can help you think through options, including a doctor, a counsellor, or a relative whose judgement you trust.
